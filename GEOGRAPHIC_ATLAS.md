# Political Geography Atlas

Implemented first phase: 16 September 2026. Route: `#geography`.

## Purpose and scope

Browse documented connections between political labels, actors, places and periods. This is distinct from the existing Research Atlas, which catalogues works, people and evidence coverage. The first collection has 10 cases across eight Middle Eastern political traditions, including North African and European connections. It is not a world-complete catalogue, a live political-control map, or eight newly scored reference profiles.

The public interface provides continent, region, country-locator, specific-place, label, relationship, period and text filters; chronological and card views; label explanations before cases; source-reading boundaries; and bibliography/encyclopedia cross-links. Filters, views and individual cases have shareable fragment URLs and survive refresh and browser history. Clipboard failure on the HTTP LAN site falls back to a selectable link.

Japan’s 2026 case is bounded to the 23 January dissolution, 8 February House election, 18 February prime-ministerial designation and 15 June Diet scrutiny of electoral access. It distinguishes the 315 LDP members elected from the later affiliation of one independent, and records 44,642 polling stations and early closing at 18,686 stations from the responsible ministry official’s committee testimony. The same transcript preserves the government’s snow-response account and legislators’ attributed concerns about short preparation, notices and accessible materials; unverified local examples are not elevated to national findings. IPU’s turnout percentage is explicitly its proportional-ballot measure. Nagatomi and Curtis provide pre-event publisher-record context only. No voter motive, blanket election-legitimacy judgment, country ideology or six-axis score is inferred; nationwide independent access evidence and event-specific book-length scholarship remain gaps.

Guyana’s 2025 case is bounded to the 1 September general and regional elections, limited recounts, final declaration and Ali’s 7 September inauguration. It distinguishes GECOM’s official seat allocation from election-observer assessments: observers generally reported peaceful, professionally administered voting and tabulation, while the EU mission documented campaign-level incumbency, media and resource inequities and the Commonwealth identified participation and accessibility gaps. The report dates to an oil-revenue and polarized political context but does not infer that income source determined individual votes. Vasciannie, Bulkan–Trotz and Hinds provide earlier scholarly contexts through 2015; their publisher records were consulted, and none covers the 2025 event. No party, ethnic community, country or electorate receives an ideological label or score.

France’s 2024–2026 case follows the dissolution and legislative election through minority-government practice, the December 2024 censure of Michel Barnier, François Bayrou’s failed September 2025 confidence vote, and the 2026 finance-law process. Official Assembly records distinguish the Article 49(1) confidence vote from Article 49(3) budget procedure and the later promulgation of Law No. 2026-103. Baranger and Beaud’s French-language constitutional-law monograph directly interprets the 2024 dissolution and crisis through 2025; Bendjaballah and Sauger provide a peer-reviewed annual analysis of 2024. Earlier Fifth Republic books are contextual only, and there is no event-specific book-length treatment covering the full sequence through 2026. The September 2026 2027-budget dispute remains an unresolved horizon. The episode is not a regime-collapse verdict, national ideological label or six-axis score; full analysis of later budget negotiations and their policy effects remains open.

The Gambia’s 2026 presidential-election entry is a pre-election snapshot reviewed 25 September, not a forecast or result. The IEC’s published calendar schedules polling for 5 December and sets out registration and nomination/campaign milestones. A later government-owned Gambia Daily report of an IEC stakeholder briefing gives different nomination and campaign dates; without an authoritative revised notice, that discrepancy remains explicit and unresolved. Two earlier political histories offer context only and predate this election. No candidate list, campaign-quality assessment, voter-motive claim, national ideology or six-axis score is inferred; the election and subsequent administrative steps remain to be documented.

Sabah’s 2025 subnational case is limited to the 29 November State Legislative Assembly election and the Chief Minister’s formal appointment effective 30 November. The official 73-seat result and turnout are separated from Puyok’s attributed interpretation and Wong’s distinct commentary; the same-author article is not counted as independent corroboration. Sabah-focused book-length work on the 2020 election and a Sarawak-focused federalism study provide context only, not direct analysis of the 2025 result. No individual voter motive, ethnic voting pattern, Sabah-wide or Malaysian ideology, or six-axis score is inferred; final election-observation and petition records remain evidence gaps.

Malaysia’s 2026 prime-ministerial term-limit entry is a federal constitutional proposal, not an enacted rule. D.R.4/2026 failed to obtain the required two-thirds support at its 2 March second reading (146 in favour; 44 did not vote; 32 absent). The latest located ministerial statement, on 3 September, said the bill was ready but scheduling was undecided. Parliamentary arguments are attributed; two book-length works and one chapter in an edited volume provide distinct institutional/election context but none studies the bill itself. No score or national ideological label is inferred, and the next parliamentary status remains to be checked.

Sweden’s 2026 entry covers the 13 September Riksdag election, the Election Authority’s final result established on 19 September, and government formation through 25 September. Official returns record 84.9% turnout and an eight-party distribution of the 349 seats. The Speaker discharged Ulf Kristersson after his request and gave Magdalena Andersson an exploratory assignment; neither action establishes a new government, and the new Riksdag had not yet convened at the review date. ODIHR did not deploy an observation mission following a needs assessment; this is not a post-election verdict. Two pre-election book-length works and a peer-reviewed study of earlier coalition formations contextualize, but do not explain, the 2026 result. No voter-motive claim, party/block ideological generalization or six-axis score is inferred; final confidence/government proceedings and event-specific scholarship remain open.

São Tomé and Príncipe’s 2026 National Assembly entry is a pre-election snapshot reviewed 25 September, two days before polling. The Constitutional Court’s docket establishes publication of party-candidacy records, while the EU observation mission’s notice establishes deployment plans only; it had not yet reported findings on this vote. The presidential election and simultaneous local and regional contests remain separate. Seibert’s history through 2005 and a 2023 comparative small-island volume provide distinct but earlier political-system contexts, not evidence about this election. The 1:110m map has no reliable locator for the islands, so the place remains accessible by Africa, Gulf of Guinea, name and case filters without borrowing another country’s polygon. No result, voter motive, national ideological label or six-axis score is inferred.

Ukraine’s 2022–2026 entry is bounded to constitutional continuity, martial-law election restrictions and election-readiness issues through 25 September 2026. It cites the Constitution and latest located martial-law extension, while distinguishing the European Commission’s earlier institutional assessment from current law and using three publisher-record-only scholarly contexts. It is not a country score or a comprehensive account of wartime politics; direct judicial interpretations and up-to-date independent monitoring remain research gaps.

Somalia’s 2026 entry is bounded to parliamentary adoption and presidential signature of the constitutional text, its Article 195 referendum procedure and the unresolved election-transition context through 25 September. The Federal Government’s characterization is distinguished from the Somali-language text’s referendum provisions and ConstitutionNet’s legal analysis; Article 195 is editorially paraphrased and lacks independent legal translation. Two earlier book-length perspectives provide context only, and are explicitly publisher-record/description-only. No referendum outcome, nationwide consensus, legal ruling, country ideology or six-axis score is inferred; further Somali-language legal review and independently sourced status evidence remain gaps.

Zambia’s 2026 entry distinguishes the ECZ declaration from the CCMG’s sample-based PRVT and from preliminary international-observer findings. CCMG’s sample placed Hichilema above the constitutional runoff threshold, but it also reported that official turnout and candidate totals fell outside estimated ranges in 30 constituencies and called for underlying tally records; its conclusion that those discrepancies did not alter the presidential winner is attributed, not converted into an independent recount or court ruling. Court closure on the final petition day, the Judiciary’s subsequent irregular-email referral, the Cabinet Office’s filing-deadline account, inauguration and opposition arrests are separately sourced. The petition’s filing status and merits remained unresolved at the review cutoff. The 2020 Zambia election volume and Tordoff–Molteno’s 1974 political history are distinct book-length contexts only; neither covers 2026, and publisher/catalogue descriptions rather than complete book texts were consulted. No electoral-legitimacy verdict, accusation about who ordered the court closure, national ideological label or six-axis score is inferred.

Ethiopia’s 2026 entry records the June 1 seventh general election, the reported NEBE announcement of 438 Prosperity Party HoPR seats, and bounded AU and IGAD preliminary observation. The result denominator differs across contemporary reporting (501 in AP; 486 certified returns in later constituency analysis), so no seat percentage is inferred. The absence of polling in Tigray and some constituencies in Amhara and Oromia, and the observers’ limited station coverage, remain explicit. Two earlier book-length works contextualize but do not analyze the election; both are publisher-record-only. No national ideology, voter consensus or six-axis score is assigned.

Guinea’s 2026 entry is limited to the 31 May National Assembly election and installation on 17 July; the simultaneous communal elections are context, not a second case. The 2025 electoral code and DGE establish the 147-seat framework and institutional dates. The final judgment and direct machine-readable return were not retrieved: IPU’s seat summary is preliminary, while the Deutsche Afrika Stiftung’s RFI-based review reports the final aligned coalition allocation and turnout, explicitly treated here as secondhand figures rather than an independent count. The March dissolution of 40 parties, boycott call, DGE/Court FRONDEG opinion and bounded WANEP preliminary account are separately attributed. AU/ECOWAS deployment notices are not observer findings, and the reported WANEP final report itself was unavailable. Camara, Adamolekun and Thiam El Hadj provide distinct earlier book-length histories/legal context via publisher records only; none covers 2026. This single electoral event does not support a six-axis reading, national ideology or voter-motive inference; detailed final returns, full judicial and observation reports, and regional participation remain gaps.

Uganda’s 2021–2026 entry has been updated beyond its original pre-final-results cutoff. The Electoral Commission now links constituency-level returns for directly elected MPs and a separate special-interest schedule; IPU Parline’s 20 May composition account reports 371 NRM seats of 529, with party and independent totals attributed to that compilation. The presidential percentages are attributed to contemporary reporting of the official declaration because a directly retrievable EC presidential-results table was not located. The February Supreme Court withdrawal of Robert Kasibante’s petition and related disclosure application ended the challenge procedurally, not through a merits finding on the allegations; Museveni’s 12 May inauguration is separately documented. The EAC report is preliminary, and the promised AU–COMESA–IGAD comprehensive final report remains unlocated. Melchiorre and Khisa’s July *African Affairs* briefing supplies a new scholarly interpretation of succession politics, but only its extract was accessible and it does not establish an accomplished transfer of power. Earlier books remain context rather than event-specific scholarship; no population consensus or six-axis score is inferred.

## Evidence model

`src/content/geography.js` holds independent place, label and case records. Each case has one primary relationship: developed, advocated, experimented, implemented, or influenced. Further meanings require another independently sourced case rather than a loose tag on a country.

- A label can connect to many places and a place to many labels. Country-locator and continental tags are many-to-many.
- Every case names an actor, historical setting, evidence statement, source IDs, reading locator, limitation, date precision, review date and confidence.
- Numeric dates support interval-overlap filtering. Approximate periods and century windows remain visibly approximate; document/study snapshots are single-year observations, not asserted start/end dates of an ideology.
- The CHP case records a 2025 primary statement. The Kurdish case records a 2020 scholarly account. Neither is relabelled as a verified present-day government or territorial-control claim just because it was consulted in 2026.
- Modern country tags do not transfer present borders backwards. Jerusalem has a dedicated historical-place locator rather than a forced sovereign assignment. Transnational regional evidence does not automatically populate every country in that region.
- No country, people or religion receives an ideological score. Related encyclopedia profiles are explicitly comparative, not aliases or exact matches.
- Political-source handling is original synthesis and links only. No source prose, translated passage or image is republished. Abstract-only and selected-section consultations remain visible. The separately licensed map geometry is documented below.

Geographic-case sources in `geography-sources.js` flow into the existing research registry, bibliography and rights records. Reused sources retain their original metadata and rights boundaries. The bibliography links back to individual cases. Recent additions include separate, bounded Colombia 2026 and Tunisia 2024 election cases, Sudan’s 2023–2025 war/civilian-protection snapshot, Côte d’Ivoire’s 2025 presidential election, Burkina Faso’s 2026 party-law dissolution and Revolution Charter transition, Mali’s 2025 party-law repeal and dissolution, Haiti’s 2026 post-Council executive transition and revised electoral timetable, Libya’s August–September 2026 Smaller Convening agreement and follow-up, Iraq’s 2025 federal election and incomplete 2026 cabinet formation, Syria’s staged 2025–2026 transitional People’s Assembly formation, Egypt’s 2025 House election and January 2026 parliamentary opening, Honduras’s 2025 general election and January 2026 transfer of office, Costa Rica’s February 2026 national election and May constitutional transition, El Salvador’s 2025 constitutional and presidential-election reform, Guatemala’s 2026 Constitutional Court renewal, Yemen’s August–September 2026 west-coast escalation and displacement, Poland’s 2025 presidential election and validation dispute, and Belarus’s January 2025 presidential election and March inauguration. The Poland record distinguishes the certified result from the limited OSCE/ODIHR observation, non-random prosecutorial sample review, judicial-validation ruling and separate ECHR precedent; it does not make a project-wide legitimacy determination or country score. The Belarus record attributes official totals to the CEC, documents the absence of a timely OSCE/ODIHR mission and limits on BHC/Viasna expert monitoring, and distinguishes UN, EU and domestic institutional positions. Its constitutional text and inauguration are recorded without resolving the prior-term legal interpretation or assigning an ideology or score. For Yemen, UN briefings, divergent state positions, IOM monitoring, WHO operational estimates, and three earlier book-length political histories are distinguished; the books contextualize but do not cover the 2026 episode. The atlas makes no territorial-control claim and assigns no country, party or population score. Official decisions, observer findings, rights reporting, political agreements and older or publisher-record-only book contexts remain distinguished. No questionnaire, archetype or axis score is changed.

## Interactive map

The atlas includes a self-hosted SVG map of 177 country/territory features, with Equal Earth projection, mouse dragging, zoom/pan buttons, a world camera reset and a Middle East camera preset. Clicking a country replaces conflicting geographic filters while preserving the selected idea, period, connection and search. Country paths and the country/territory select use the same stable IDs; existing six country IDs and all earlier links remain valid. Uncatalogued areas return explicit research gaps rather than invented labels. Changing filters updates highlighted countries and case counts locally. Camera changes do not change the research selection.

The city/region layer provides rounded editorial navigation anchors for Baghdad, Damascus, Jerusalem and northern Syria. They are not surveyed locations, territorial extents or historical sovereignty claims. Dedicated controls preserve Ottoman-network and Arab-world cases without assigning them wholesale to a country. Region controls filter existing catalogue groupings; camera windows are not drawn as regional borders.

Country paths have roving keyboard focus (arrow keys, Home/End, Enter/Space). Native country and place selects provide equivalent navigation, including tiny polygons. Touch retains page scrolling; zoom/pan buttons work without dragging. Reduced-motion settings disable map animations. Geographic URLs, refresh and back/forward navigation synchronize both map selection and results; camera position itself is transient.

Map data: `world-atlas` 2.0.2 `countries-110m.json`, derived from Natural Earth 4.1.0, 1:110m. This fixed, simplified dataset omits some small areas and is not a current control map. Names and disputed boundaries follow that dataset; neither constitutes a sovereignty endorsement. Its version, limitations, original public-domain data terms and software notices are linked in the public map. See `public/map-data-notices.txt`, [Natural Earth terms](https://www.naturalearthdata.com/about/terms-of-use/) and [boundary policy](https://www.naturalearthdata.com/about/disputed-boundaries-policy/). No political scores, cases or existing bibliography records were changed for this feature.

## Performance and privacy

The versioned index is a small static module; filtering and sorting happen locally. The atlas interface, map geometry, projection code and CSS load on demand. The map adds pinned `d3-geo`, `topojson-client` and `world-atlas` dependencies; Vite bundles the selected 110m dataset and required code. There are no per-filter server calls, new database/cache services, map-service tokens, external tiles, tracking or location requests. Fragment URLs store only user-chosen browsing filters, not questionnaire answers.

The pre-existing application still eagerly includes its large encyclopedia/bibliography bundle and requests Google Fonts. Those existing font requests are outside the map's self-hosting claim. This change does not claim to have split all articles into individual downloads; that remains a separate performance task. Map tiles, street-level detail and speculative historical polygons remain out of scope.

## Validation

`validate-geography.js` runs in the build validator and `test-geography.mjs` runs in the content suite. Checks cover stable/unique IDs, resolved sources and encyclopedia links, bibliography backlinks, chronology, explicit uncertainty and the absence of invented scored profiles. Unit/browser tests cover filters, accent-insensitive search, empty gaps, chronology, malformed URLs, refresh, history, deep links, clipboard fallback, mobile layout and serious/critical accessibility violations.

## Next research and product phases

1. Deepen the starter cases with primary texts and independent implementation studies. In particular, the Nasserist example needs policy-specific evidence beyond its current abstract-level frame.
2. Add separate Iraqi and Syrian Ba’athist regime cases; historical Kemalism; distinct Islamic modernist and Islamist traditions; and Labor, Revisionist and Religious Zionist traditions. None should be silently merged into a generic religious or nationalist score.
3. Extend coverage beyond the pilot with the same actor/date/source requirements. Zero cases is a research gap, not evidence of historical absence.
4. For any present-day implementation claim, define the precise institution and scope, check current primary evidence and independent scholarship/reporting, and record an explicit as-of date and follow-up review policy.
5. Extend the map only with reviewed geographic data and clear boundary conventions: consider newer datasets, tiny-country locators and independently sourced historical-region overlays. Retain non-map browsing and distinguish research coverage from political control.

This feature does not change the existing one-entry encyclopedia research automation or close issue #14. Deployment and worldwide coverage are separate from completion of this first implementation.
